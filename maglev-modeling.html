Introduction

Why maglev, why differential equations

EDS (electrodynamic suspension) maglev trains use superconductive magnets to repel a magnetic track, allowing them to levitate. This repulsion becomes stronger at high speeds, per Faraday's law. This project models velocity, levitation height, and load size for a train based on the Japanese L0 series Maglev (603 kph / 167.5 m/s top speed), then uses the model to derive real-world safety parameters: maximum passenger load and safe voltage-ramp rate.

The derivation manipulates fundamental electromagnetic equations — the Fourier series and Lorentz force law — and Newton's laws to arrive at the ODEs describing the train's behavior. After identifying key constants, Euler's Method and Runge–Kutta (RK4) are applied to numerically approximate these ODEs, and their accuracy is compared.

Euler's Method

Estimates the next point on a curve by taking the current value and adding the local slope times a small step. Inaccurate at larger step sizes, since errors compound with each iteration.

$$f(x_1) = f(x_0) + \frac{df}{dx_0}\Delta x$$

Runge–Kutta (RK4)

Incorporates the slope over the whole interval rather than just the starting point, using four weighted slope estimates (k₁–k₄) per step — far more accurate at the same step size.

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{1}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$$

Governing Physics

The Meissner Effect & Levitation Force

EDS levitation is based on the Meissner effect, where a superconductor forces the magnetic field inside it to zero, creating a surface current that repels the field.

Lorentz Force Law

Gives the repulsion between the superconductor's induced current and the track's magnetic field.

$$F_{Repulsion} = \int J \times B(x,z)\, dV$$

Halbach Array

Magnets are arranged in a Halbach array (see hero diagram above), concentrating the field on one side rather than splitting it evenly between both faces. The periodic field is approximated with a Fourier series:

$$B(x,z) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big[A_n\cos(k_nx) + B_n\sin(k_nx)\big]e^{-k_nz}$$

Second London Equation

Relates superconducting current density to the field:

$$\nabla \times J = -\frac{n_se^2}{m}B$$

Substituting Ampère's law ($J_s = \nabla\times B/\mu_0$) in for $J$, applying the vector identity $\nabla\times\nabla\times F = \nabla(\nabla\cdot F) - \nabla^2F$, and using $\nabla\cdot B = 0$, the field equation reduces to a second-order linear ODE:

$$\frac{d^2B(z')}{dz'^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda_L^2}B(z')$$

Assuming an exponential solution $B(z') = B_0e^{rz'}$ gives the characteristic equation $r^2 = 1/\lambda_L^2$. Since the field must decay — not grow — with depth, only the negative root is physical:

$$B(z') = B_0e^{-z'/\lambda_L}$$

London Penetration Depth

Integrating $B(z')$ to get vector potential, then current density, then integrating over the superconductor's depth $d_{sc}$ gives the overall surface current:

$$J_{overall} = \int_0^{d_{sc}}J\,dz' = \frac{B_0}{\mu_0}\left(1 - e^{-d_{sc}/\lambda_L}\right)$$

The penetration depth $\lambda$ itself depends on train velocity — transitioning from the classic "skin effect" at low speed to the London depth at high speed:

$$\lambda(t) = \sqrt{\lambda_L^2 + \frac{\tau}{\mu\sigma\pi v(t)}\left(\frac{v_0}{v(t)}\right)^n}$$

Combining $J$ and $B$, applying Parseval's theorem to collapse the Fourier integral, and factoring out effective working area $A_{area}$ gives the final repulsion force:

$$F_{Repulsion} = \frac{\left(1-e^{-d/\lambda(t)}\right)}{2\mu_0}A_{area}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-2k_nz}\left(A_n^2+B_n^2\right)$$

Applying Newton's second law with gravity gives the levitation ODE:

$$\frac{d^2z}{dt^2} = \frac{F_{Repulsion}}{m} - 9.807\,\frac{m}{s^2}$$

Propulsion

Linear Synchronous Motor

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law & Motor Current

The motor's forward thrust comes from the Lorentz law applied to the motor winding, $F_{motor}(t) = k_tI(t)$. Solving the motor's Kirchhoff-voltage equation for current — using an integrating factor — and substituting back in gives:

$$F_{motor}(t) = k_t\frac{1}{R_m}(V - k_ev)\left(1-e^{-\frac{R_m}{L_m}t}\right)$$

Total drag combines aerodynamic drag and eddy (magnetic damping) drag:

$$F_{drag} = C_1v(t) + \frac{1}{2}\rho C_dA_fv(t)^2$$

Modeling voltage as a smooth exponential ramp $V(t) = V_{max}(1-e^{-t/r})$ and applying Newton's second law gives the full velocity ODE, driven by $F_{motor}(t) - F_{drag}(t)$ divided by train mass $m$.

Constant Fitting

Solving for the Unknown Constants

Several constants — $k_t$, $A_{area}$, $v_0$, $n$ — aren't published anywhere. They were inferred by forcing the model to match known real-world behavior of the L0. This was the part of the project that felt the least like "plugging into a formula" and the most like engineering judgment.

Motor Constant & Rm

Assuming an ideal motor ($k_t \approx k_e \approx k$), the steady-state balance at top speed gives one equation with two unknowns. The second condition: the real L0 reaches top speed in ~4 minutes, so $R_m$ was tuned until simulated spin-up matched.

Rm ≈ 2.3 Ω, k ≈ 114.22 — verified to 8 significant figures.

Effective Working Area

At maximum levitation, net vertical force is zero: $F_{Repulsion} = mg$. Plugging in the train's mass and solving for the one unknown gives:

$$A_{area} \approx 76.44\ m^2$$

Skin-Effect Decay Rate

Bracketed by two conditions: liftoff at 30 m/s, and skin depth shrinking to sub-nanometer scale at top speed (168 m/s). The smallest integer satisfying both:

$$n = 11$$

Results

Euler vs. RK4

Velocity

Both methods track almost identically here, since velocity changes smoothly with no sharp local curvature for Euler's method to mishandle. The curve plateaus near 167.5 m/s within the L0's real ~4-minute spin-up window.

Levitation Height

Euler's method uses the acceleration at a single point per step, over-expressing the slight oscillation from counteracting repulsion/gravity forces — visibly diverging. RK4 averages the slope across the interval, settling smoothly at ≈0.1 m.

This gap is global error: RK4's error scales with step size to the 4th power, versus 1st power for Euler — so RK4 stays accurate at step sizes where Euler visibly breaks down.

Application

Discussion & Real-World Safety Parameters

Voltage Ramp Rate

The maximum passenger-safe acceleration is ~1.0 m/s². Solving for the ramp constant $r$ that keeps acceleration under this limit gives $r \geq 85$.

Load Limit

Higher mass lowers the levitation height. At the moderate-danger threshold (9 cm), the overall mass limit is 460,000 kg — with the train itself at 360,000 kg, that leaves 100,000 kg for passengers and storage.
